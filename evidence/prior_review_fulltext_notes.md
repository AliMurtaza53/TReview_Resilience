[Has22]:
- **Type and angle:** Bibliometric analysis combined with a review of research on road-transport-network vulnerability under natural hazards. It charts the field’s evolution, contributions, keywords, and assessment approaches, rather than testing one vulnerability method (Introduction; §§2–4, PDF pp. 2, 14–16).
- **Search and coverage:** Scopus; data mined **30 March–5 May 2021**; publication years **1975–2020**. Queries combined transport vulnerability/vulnerable and natural-hazard/disaster terms with road/transport network, infrastructure, or system terms; the initial broader search also included “disaster” and “hazard.” The authors report 3,898 initial records, 650 selected, then 635 retained after screening (Methods; Table 1, PDF p. 4).  
  Table 1 gives the three search-string stages and record counts (3,898 → 650 → 635).

Table 7 is a synthesis of selected papers, not the complete corpus; the references are not an inclusion list. **No inclusion list was extracted in this reading.**
- **Dimensions/taxonomy:** Six assessment types: **system performance, system demand, system resiliency, system capacity, area-based, system geometry** (§4, p. 14; Table 7, pp. 15–16). The Conclusion also groups research by pre-, during-, and post-disaster focus (p. 17).
- **Coverage of assessment issues:** Table 7 catalogs hazard types, methods, exposure, data, outputs, and adaptation interventions. Examples cover floods, landslides, earthquakes/seismic hazards, and multiple hazards; network demand, links/nodes, travel time, accessibility, and capacity; disruption, evacuation, restoration, and resilience; and consequences including travel delay, economic/logistics costs, and service loss. These are summarized examples, not a uniform evaluation framework.

- **Optimization:** Reports decision problems and approaches including budget-constrained vulnerability reduction using simulated annealing, evacuation routing (CCRP), and multiobjective shelter location. [Reb21]:
**Rebally et al. (2021):** A PRISMA-guided review asks how flood-impact assessment methods are used on transportation networks, and how the assessed impacts relate to temporal and spatial scales. It is not framed as a comparison of prior reviews. (Review Methodology, p. 6; Introduction, pp. 5–6.)

**Search and coverage:** Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, conference and university portals; search strings included “flood impacts,” “flood temporal and spatial scale,” “flood traffic model,” “network disruption,” and “flood vulnerability,” among others. Search accessed April 2021; most reviewed publications were through 2020, with a few from 2021. No exact publication start date is reported. (Review Methodology, p. 6.) The authors selected 49 papers: 30 for critical assessment and 19 for terminology/literature survey. Supplementary Table 1 is said to contain greater detail; it is a candidate inclusion list for an overlap matrix, unlike the bibliography, but **I did not extract its entries here**. (Review Methodology, p. 6.)

**Dimensions and treatment:** Impact categories are direct/indirect × tangible/intangible; methods are probabilistic, analytical, fuzzy inference, graph theory, empirical, and hybrid. Time categories are short (0–2 weeks), medium (2 weeks–6 months), and long (>6 months); spatial categories are community (<5 km), urban (5–200 km), and regional (≥200 km). (pp. 2, 5; Figure 3, p. 9.) Hazard treatment includes probability, intensity/frequency, depth, duration, and flow characteristics. Network analyses include road/highway networks, traffic data/models, and graph nodes/links. Recovery is linked to short-term response; adaptation and transition to longer horizons. Tangible impacts may be monetized; intangible impacts are described as difficult to value. (pp. 2–5, 7–8.)

**Optimization and appraisal:** Optimization, assignment algorithms, and prescriptive decision problems are not reported as review dimensions; interventions are discussed as recommendations, not systematically optimized. The review discusses data/model uncertainty and accuracy limitations, but reports no systematic validation or equity assessment. (pp. 5, 7–8, 11–12.)

**Guidance/limitations:** Authors call for long-term assessment, methods valuing indirect/intangible impacts, post-flood recovery analysis, multimodal studies, and evaluation of resilient-network changes. They note data scarcity/uncertainty, scope limited to surface transport, possible missed recent work, and inadequate attention to cascading effects. (pp. 5–6, 11–12.)

[Fat15]:
**Review type and angle.** Faturechi & Miller-Hooks is a comprehensive, classificatory literature review of how transportation-system performance is assessed and managed when future hazards cause physical infrastructure damage. Its angle is organizing the literature by performance concept, life-cycle phase, measures, uncertainty treatment, and methods (“Study Scope,” pp. 2–3; “Conclusions and Insights,” p. 10). The authors report **approximately 200** articles/reports.

**Search and coverage.** Databases, search strings, search dates, and a precise publication cutoff: **NR**. The scope is mainly work since 2000, with pioneering late-1990s work included (p. 2). Hazards are natural, operational/technological, or intentional; “disaster” entails extensive physical damage. Evacuation, humanitarian logistics, well-being/environmental effects, and component material/structural studies are excluded (p. 2).

**Inclusion records.** No single exhaustive master inclusion list is identified. **Tables 2–7 provide categorized reference inventories**, distinct from the full bibliography (pp. 4–10); they could support a category-based overlap matrix, but not necessarily a complete one. I inspected these tables in this reading; I did not extract their citations into an overlap matrix.

**Taxonomies and treatment.** Dimensions actually used: performance concepts (risk, vulnerability, reliability, robustness, flexibility, survivability, resilience); qualitative/quantitative; life-cycle phase (mitigation, preparedness, response, recovery); MOEs (functional—travel time/distance, throughput/capacity, accessibility—topological, economic); uncertainty modeling (scenario, simulation, probability distribution, worst-case, historical); and methodology (analytical, simulation, deterministic/stochastic optimization) (Tables 1–7, pp. 3–10). Networks are treated through component/system performance, flows, assignments, accessibility, and graph measures. Recovery is a life-cycle phase. Consequences include operational degradation and economic loss; the authors say no reviewed work presents/discusses quantitative economic measures for disaster management (p. 7).

Optimization covers assessment and prescriptive design, protection, resource allocation, and repair/recovery scheduling; assignment/user-choice models are also included (Tables 6–7, pp. 9–10). Uncertainty is explicitly categorized; validation and equity are not review dimensions or explicitly assessed.

**Guidance/limitations.** Authors note ~70% of publications assess coping ability and >90% of management studies address only one life-cycle phase; they call for more qualitative-study categorization and note growing attention to uncertainty, dependencies, system-level performance, and sensitivity analysis (p. 10). Most reviewed work concerns surface transportation (p. 10). No other explicit limitations are stated.

Table 1 summarizes the seven performance concepts and their definitions, useful for comparing terminology.

[Lam26]:
**Lam26** (Lamei et al., 2026; *Infrastructures* 11, 130, doi:10.3390/infrastructures11040130) is a PRISMA-guided systematic review (Methods, §§2.1–2.3, pp. 6–9). Its angle is disruption modeling in multimodal freight, organized around methods (RQ1), interdependencies/cascades/recovery (RQ2), and validation/calibration/testing (RQ3).

The authors searched Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and TRID; Table 2 gives database-specific strings based on resilience AND freight AND multimodal terms. English publications from Jan. 2015–Dec. 2025 were eligible; search execution dates: **NR** (§2.1, pp. 6–7). They included **21** studies. Table 3 is an explicit record-level inclusion list, distinct from the full bibliography (§3.1, p. 10).

Coded dimensions include disruption type/representation, modeling method, cascading class, temporal class, recovery actions, uncertainty, data, calibration/validation, real-event use, and performance/resilience metrics (§2.3, p. 9; Tables 4–6). Hazards are grouped as natural, operational/infrastructure, human-induced, and pandemic; network effects include node/link failure, capacity loss, and occasional demand shocks (§3.2, pp. 11–13). Most analyses are static; recovery is often a decision (rerouting/repair), not a trajectory; Misra and Padgett [1] is the clearest time-evolving recovery example (§3.3, pp. 14–15). Consequences are valued with heterogeneous cost, time, flow/service, economic-loss, and recovery measures (Table 6, p. 16).

Optimization is dominant: prescriptive routing, location, recovery, and planning, using MIP/MILP/MINLP, stochastic/robust methods, and heuristics. Assignment/network-science methods assess robustness; simulation/ML capture operational propagation or prediction (§3.2.3, pp. 11–13). Validation and uncertainty are assessed; equity is not assessed as a coded dimension. The authors recommend standardized disruption/metric reporting, calibration and historical backtesting, benchmarks, cascade and recovery-trajectory modeling, stronger uncertainty treatment, and broader modal/regional testing. Limitations include the 21-study sample and U.S./road–rail skew; they note limited calibration/backtesting and heterogeneous metrics (§§2.2, 3.1, 3.4, 4; pp. 8–10, 15–18).

Table 3 lists the 21 included records and can support an overlap matrix for Lam26. No other review’s inclusion list was extracted here; its full bibliography is not itself an inclusion list.

[Ver24]:
**Ver24 (Verschuur et al., 2024; PLOS Climate 3(4): e0000331).**

- **Review type and angle:** Cross-sector comparative review of recent quantitative methods for climate-risk analysis across water, telecom, social, transport and energy infrastructure; asks what analytical developments and cross-cutting gaps exist. The authors say they rely on expert judgment and synthesize literature into sectoral research strands (Introduction, PDF p. 2).
- **Search and coverage:** Databases, search strings, search dates, and publication-year coverage: **NR** (Introduction, p. 2). Included-study count: **NR**. No explicit included-studies list/table is reported; S1 Table catalogs events and S2 Table operational thresholds, not included papers (Supporting information, p. 14). The references are a bibliography, not an inclusion list.
- **Dimensions/taxonomies actually used:** Infrastructure sectors/subsectors; hazard–infrastructure combinations rated by **vulnerability and impact**; and Dawson et al.’s **four tiers**—asset, network, interdependent/cascading, systemic risk (Fig. 1, p. 3; “Quantifying climate risks,” pp. 5–6; Fig. 3, p. 6).
- **Analytical treatment:** Hazards include single and multiple hazards, with multi-hazard interactions identified as underdeveloped (pp. 11–12). Network analysis includes service disruption, dependencies, cascading failures, and flows; transport systemic analysis needs passenger/freight flows (pp. 5–7). Temporal issues include operational downtime and recovery, but recovery modelling is sector-dependent and understudied for health/education (pp. 4, 10). Consequences include asset damage, service interruption, economic and wider social, health, fiscal, and environmental impacts; higher-tier and non-monetary metrics remain limited (pp. 5, 13–14).
- **Optimization:** Water studies use decision-making under uncertainty to compare robust/adaptive investments; energy power-flow models often optimize system operation. No common review taxonomy of algorithms, assignment methods, or prescriptive interventions is presented (pp. 9–10, 13).
- **Assessment and guidance:** Validation difficulty, uncertainty, and equity are explicitly discussed as gaps (pp. 12–13). Guidance: improve multi-hazard/interdependency modelling, data and validation, cross-sector flow/disruption metrics, equity, and impact measures (pp. 11–14). The stated methodological basis is expert judgment, not a reported systematic search (p. 2). **No inclusion list was extracted here**, so this review cannot directly populate a study-level overlap matrix.

[Nir24]:
**Review type and angle.** A systematic literature review and data collection: it assembles physical fragility/vulnerability curves for CI assets exposed to flooding, earthquakes, windstorms and landslides, standardizes them, and identifies coverage gaps (Secs. 1–2, pp. 4341–45).

**Search and coverage.** Google Scholar; 125 combinations of hazard, CI and vulnerability terms (Table 1, p. 4343; Appendix A, pp. 4359–62). Search ran 22 January–March 2023; no publication-year or geographic search limit. English- and Dutch-language research only. The search screened 31,250 records and included 95 references (Sec. 2.1, pp. 4342–44); curve publication dates shown span 1984–2023 (Fig. 4, p. 4357). The paper does not report a database-by-database search beyond Google Scholar.

**Included-study list and taxonomy.** No explicit included-study roster/table distinct from the general bibliography is provided. Appendix A lists search strings, not included studies. The associated Zenodo database’s Tables D1–D3 are referenced but were not extracted in this reading. Thus, the article itself does not supply an extracted inclusion list for a review-overlap matrix. Taxonomies used: seven CI systems; four hazards; hazard intensity, exposed element, vulnerability/fragility characteristics, cost feature, uncertainty, derivation method, geography and source details (Secs. 2.2–2.3, pp. 4344–46; Fig. 3, p. 4346).

**Topics and methods.** It discusses hazard measures (including flood depth versus flow velocity), and asset/system-level curves, but not network topology or flow assignment (Secs. 3–4.3, pp. 4346–59). It records some temporal vulnerability (e.g. cumulative landslide displacement), but does not model recovery; it flags repair time, disruption and interdependencies as future extensions (Sec. 5, p. 4359). Consequence valuation covers physical damage/MDR and cost data, not a review of damage-to-loss models (Eq. 1; Secs. 2.2–2.3, pp. 4344–45). Optimization, algorithms, assignment and prescriptive intervention are not reviewed.

**Assessment and guidance.** Uncertainty ranges are catalogued where available; the authors did not quality-check curves. Equity assessment is not reported (Secs. 2.2, 5, pp. 4344, 4359). Guidance: prioritize telecommunications; add hazards, assets (e.g. bridges), asset characteristics, multi-intensity and multi-hazard curves, and operational/interdependency outcomes. Stated limitations include possible omitted studies, selective reliance on prior reviews, and the need to consider curve resolution, transferability and adaptability (Sec. 5, p. 4359).

*Source: Nirandjan et al. (2024), “Review article: Physical vulnerability database for critical infrastructure hazard risk assessments – a systematic review and data collection,”* Natural Hazards and Earth System Sciences, 24, 4341–4368, https://doi.org/10.5194/nhess-24-4341-2024.
